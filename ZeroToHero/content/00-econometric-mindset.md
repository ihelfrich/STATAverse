## Overview
Econometrics is not software-first. It is a decision process: define the outcome, identify variation,
map assumptions to estimators, and explain results in plain language. This module builds the mental
model that you will reuse in every method that follows.

## Learning goals
- Translate a research question into a testable model.
- Identify the source of variation that makes estimation possible.
- Recognize which estimator matches a data type.
- Practice the Try -> Predict -> Check -> Reflect loop.

## The four core questions
Use these four prompts before touching any estimator:

1) **Outcome**: What exactly are we trying to explain?
2) **Variation**: Where does the change in the outcome come from?
3) **Mechanism**: What variables represent the story or theory?
4) **Assumptions**: What must be true for the estimator to be valid?

## Step 2: Identify the data generating process
The data generating process (DGP) is your mental map for how outcomes are created.

**Core idea:**
\(\text{Outcome} = \text{Systematic part} + \text{Noise}\)

<details class="math-toggle algebra">
  <summary>Show algebra form</summary>
  <div class="math-block">
    We often start with the linear model:

    \[y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + u_i\]

    where \(y_i\) is the outcome (sales), \(x_i\) is a predictor (promotion), and \(u_i\) is everything
    you did not measure.
  </div>
</details>

<details class="math-toggle linalg">
  <summary>Show linear algebra form</summary>
  <div class="math-block">
    Stack all observations into vectors and matrices:

    \[\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\beta + \mathbf{u}\]

    \(\mathbf{y}\) is an \(n \times 1\) outcome vector, \(\mathbf{X}\) is an \(n \times k\) matrix of predictors,
    and \(\beta\) is a \(k \times 1\) vector of coefficients.
  </div>
</details>

## Step 3: Match outcome to estimator
<div class="decision-grid">
  <div class="decision-card">
    <h3>Continuous outcome</h3>
    <p>Use OLS first. Then check assumptions and robustness.</p>
    <span class="pill">Example: sales, profit</span>
  </div>
  <div class="decision-card">
    <h3>Binary outcome</h3>
    <p>Use logit/probit. Interpret marginal effects, not raw coefficients.</p>
    <span class="pill">Example: churn yes/no</span>
  </div>
  <div class="decision-card">
    <h3>Count outcome</h3>
    <p>Use Poisson/negative binomial. Check for overdispersion.</p>
    <span class="pill">Example: defects per shift</span>
  </div>
  <div class="decision-card">
    <h3>Time to event</h3>
    <p>Use hazard models. Validate proportional hazards when relevant.</p>
    <span class="pill">Example: time to failure</span>
  </div>
</div>

## Why this matters
Econometrics is not about memorizing estimators. It is about making defensible choices that link
business questions, data, and assumptions. When you can explain each choice in plain language,
you are ready to build models that publish and persuade.

## Self-test
1) What is the DGP in one sentence?
2) Why is variation necessary for estimation?
3) Which estimator would you start with for a binary outcome?

Answer key (short)
- Q1: A map of how outcomes are generated by predictors plus noise.
- Q2: Without variation, you cannot identify relationships.
- Q3: Logit or probit, then interpret marginal effects.
